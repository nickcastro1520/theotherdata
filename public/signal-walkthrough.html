  1. 1. The idea

    Amazon ships a staggering number of boxes. Boxes are made from paperboard. So if U.S. mills are making more paperboard than a year ago, more goods are probably moving through warehouses. If they’re making less, fewer packages may be in the system. It’s a hunch, not a fact, and the rest of this page is about testing it.

    Why output and not box prices? Prices can rise just because pulp or energy got more expensive. Output measures volume, which is closer to what we care about. Box prices still appear on the Amazon page, but only as context.

  2. 2. Where the data comes from

    The Federal Reserve publishes an industrial production index for paperboard mills every month. It’s free and public: series on . An index just means “output compared with a base year,” so 100 is the base year’s level and the direction matters more than the number itself.

  3. 3. How it’s pulled on a schedule

    A GitHub Actions job runs every 4 hours. It asks FRED for the latest numbers, saves them as a small data file, and commits it. That commit redeploys the site automatically, so the page is never more than a few hours behind the source. Government data comes out slowly, which is why the newest reading is from .

  5. 5. How the correlation scout fits in

    This signal started as a hand-picked idea, not a scout find. The scout is a separate monthly job that tests lots of offbeat public series against stock prices and ranks the strongest links, checking whether the data moves before the price. It’s a brainstorming tool: with that many comparisons, some links show up by pure luck, so a scout idea still has to pass the same honest test below before anyone should trust it.

  6. 6. Testing it honestly

    The site rewinds to each month from to , and asks what this signal would have said using only data that was public by then. It assumes a -day delay before each reading came out, so it never peeks at the future. Then it checks what Amazon’s stock did over the next month.

    Across checkpoints the signal pointed up times, down times, and was quiet times. Quiet months aren’t scored, which leaves calls.

  7. 7. The fairer test: beating the market

    In a rising market almost everything goes up, so “the stock went up” is too easy a test. The tougher one is whether Amazon did better than the S&P 500 over the same month when the signal pointed up, or worse when it pointed down.

  8. 8. What it means for Amazon

    Amazon’s combined lean made scored calls in the backtest. It was right on direction of the time and beat the S&P 500 of the time. That’s encouraging, but the sample is small, and every call so far has been a tailwind. A clue, not a forecast.
